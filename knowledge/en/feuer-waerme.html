Fire, warmth & shelter

Staying warm mainly means: stay dry, dress in layers, insulate from the ground and wind – and never breathe exhaust fumes.

Does not replace training. In an emergency, call 112 first, or keep trying. This page deliberately gives no medication doses – ask a doctor or pharmacist.

Right now: how to stay warm

  1. Get out of wind and wet. Go into a building, under a roof or behind a windbreak.
  2. Change wet clothing. Wet clothing cools you down heavily. Put on dry clothes.
  3. Dress in layers. Several thin layers keep you warmer than one thick one. Cover head, neck, hands and feet.
  4. Insulate from the ground. Never sit or lie directly on cold ground. Use a mat, cardboard, blankets or branches.
  5. Eat and drink something warm. The body needs energy to produce heat.
  6. Heat and use only one room. Doors closed. But ventilate regularly.
Carbon monoxide (CO) kills silently

CO is invisible and odourless. It is produced by any incomplete combustion: charcoal, gas, wood, petrol. The first signs are headache and dizziness – they are easily mistaken for tiredness.

  • Never run a barbecue, gas stove or patio heater in a flat, tent or car.
  • Never use a gas hob or oven for heating.
  • With headache, dizziness, nausea: get into the fresh air immediately, take others with you, call 112.
  • A battery-powered CO alarm increases safety.

How heat is lost

The body loses heat in four ways: to cold air, to cold objects, through wind and through evaporation of moisture. That is why these rules help:

  • Layering principle: Several loose layers. There is air between them, and that insulates. Tight clothing restricts circulation.
  • Do not get sweaty: Sweat makes clothing wet. When exerting yourself, open your jacket or take off your hat before you get hot.
  • Keep dry: Wet clothing loses its insulation. Change wet socks and gloves first.
  • Keep clean: Matted, dirty clothing insulates worse.
  • Avoid wind: Wind cools the skin far more than the thermometer shows. A windbreak often does more than an extra jacket.
  • Cover your head: A hat and scarf are quickly put on and very effective.
Show original – US manual FM 3-05.70, ch. 5

When at rest, you lose as much as 80 percent of your body heat to the ground.

The figure "80 percent" is a rule of thumb from the manual. What matters is the message: the ground draws heat away very quickly.

Warning signs of hypothermia

Shivering is the first sign. If thinking becomes sluggish, someone behaves confusedly or the strong shivering stops, it is serious. Then:

  1. Bring the person out of wind and wet. Take off wet clothing, wrap them in dry things.
  2. Insulate from below, wrap them in blankets or a sleeping bag.
  3. Only if the person is awake: give warm, sweetened drinks. Do not force anything into an unconscious person.
  4. Get help: 112. See also First aid.

A hot bath is not a home remedy: the manual warns that rapid warming can endanger the heart.

Staying warm at home (heating fails)

In a power outage many heating systems do not run. The BBK (Federal Office for Civil Protection and Disaster Assistance) advises: dress warmly, use blankets and choose a single room to stay in.

  • Small room, doors closed. That keeps the warmth in. Ideally a room with little exterior wall.
  • Get sleeping bags and blankets out of the cupboard. Hat and socks in bed too.
  • Huddle together with others. Several people help warm a room.
  • Air briefly at regular intervals, even in winter. This applies especially when candles are burning. Never leave candles unattended. LED lamps and torches are better.
  • Stove or fireplace: Only use if the chimney and flue are in order. The BBK recommends getting advice from specialists on heating alternatives. Gas heaters need an oxygen depletion sensor and an ignition safety device.
  • Risk of frost: After a long outage in frost, watch the water pipes. Leaks sometimes show up only later.

Improvised heaters (for example made of tealights and flowerpots) are deliberately not recommended here. Their safety is not proven.

Shelter outdoors

Outdoors, a shelter should be small. The US manual describes it like this: large enough to protect you, small enough to hold your body heat.

Show original – US manual FM 3-05.70, ch. 5

A shelter must be large enough to protect you and small enough to contain your body heat, especially in cold climates.

What matters:

  • Location: Not in hollows, gullies or stream beds. Cold air collects there. Avoid places with loose branches or rocks above you.
  • Wind protection: Back to the wind. Rock, dense bushes or fallen trees help.
  • Protection from below: A thick layer of leaves, fir branches or dry grass under you. Better too much than too little.
  • Roof: It must keep out rain. It does not have to be big.

Two basic designs:

  • Lean-to: A long pole lies horizontally between two trees. Branches leaned at an angle form the wall. On top go branches, leaves or needles from the bottom up like roof tiles. The back faces into the wind.
  • Debris hut: A ridgepole supports a low frame. A very thick layer of leaves goes on top. Inside there is only room for your own body. The manual calls it one of the best simple designs for warmth.

Emergency blanket: Wrap it tightly around you so that hardly any air escapes. It should not lie tight against the body. The foil does not insulate by itself: clothing, blankets or a mat belong underneath. It burns easily and is not suitable as a fire blanket.

Making fire

Legal situation in Germany

Open fire in forests and near forests is mostly forbidden in Germany. The federal states set the rules. In North Rhine-Westphalia, for example, there is a ban in the forest and within 100 metres of the forest edge. When there is a wildfire risk, open fire is particularly dangerous everywhere. In a real emergency, self-protection comes first; otherwise observe the bans. Use permitted fire places and ask the forestry office or the municipality beforehand.

The three stages

Fire needs fuel, heat and air. Build it up in three stages:

StageWhatExamples
TinderCatches with just a sparkDry grass, birch bark, fine wood shavings, cotton wool, pocket lint
KindlingThin and dryTwigs as thick as a matchstick, thinly split wood
FirewoodBurns for a long timeDry branches, split logs, gradually thicker

Everything must be dry. Collect more than you think you need and lay it within reach. Dead wood still hanging on the tree is usually drier than wood on the ground.

Step by step

  1. Choose the place: Dry, sheltered from wind, far from trees, tent or building. Not under branches.
  2. Clear the ground: Remove leaves, grass and needles. The manual recommends a circle of at least 1 metre in diameter. A rim of earth or stones helps. Do not use wet or flaky (shale-like) stones: they can burst in the heat.
  3. Build it: Tinder in the middle, kindling over it like a tent. Have firewood ready.
  4. Light it: With a match, lighter or ferro rod. A ferro rod only produces sparks. For that you need very dry tinder.
  5. Add fuel slowly: First fine, then thicker wood. Always leave air between the pieces.
  6. Never leave it unattended. Wind carries sparks far.

Protect matches: Pack them in a sealed tin or a freezer bag. Keep spares in a second place.

In wind: Build a pit or a windbreak from stones or wood. In strong wind, better not to make a fire. Flying sparks can set forests and fields alight.

Putting out a fire

  1. Put water, sand or earth on the fire.
  2. Stir the embers and put them out again. Hold your flat hand over the ashes without touching them: no heat may be felt any more.
  3. Cover the ashes with earth. Leave only when everything is cold.

Fire safety and security

  • Grease fire: never put it out with water. It causes a grease explosion. Put a lid on and switch off the hob.
  • Fire blanket: Simple household blankets are only of limited effectiveness for grease fires and fires on people. A suitable fire extinguisher is better. An emergency blanket is not a fire blanket.
  • Fire extinguishers should hang within reach. Learn beforehand how it works.
  • Never pour methylated spirits or petrol into a burning or smouldering fire. This causes flash fires and sudden bursts of flame.
  • Barbecue only outdoors. Charcoal barbecues and gas stoves never belong in a flat, garage or tent: risk of suffocation from CO.
  • Keep children and animals away from the fire. Do not hold clothes or hair over the flame.
  • In case of fire: Your own safety first. Leave the flat, close the door, call 112.
