Navigation without GPS

Lost? Stop, think, orient yourself, then plan. Map and compass are the safest – sun and stars only give a rough direction.

Does not replace training. In an emergency, call 112 first, or keep trying. This page deliberately gives no medication doses – ask a doctor or pharmacist.

Right now: you are lost

Remember STOP. It is a widely used memory aid in outdoor training. It matches the principle of the US manual: think first, then act.

  1. S – Stop. Sit down. Breathe. Do not keep walking aimlessly.
  2. T – Think. Where were you last sure of your position? How long have you walked since then? Do you have water, light, warm clothing?
  3. O – Orient. Map, compass, phone, landmarks, sounds (road, railway, water, bells).
  4. P – Plan. Decide: stay or go? If there are two or more of you, decide together.
Show original – US manual FM 3-05.70, ch. 1

Don’t move just for the sake of taking action.

Stay if people are looking for you, or you are injured or exhausted, if it is getting dark or if you are not sure. Then give signals (see below) and protect yourself from the cold: Fire & warmth.

Go only if you know a safe direction. Keep your course: do not walk in circles, do not take shortcuts. In Germany, paths, roads, railway lines, pylons or streams usually lead to settlements soon.

If you have phone reception, call 112 and describe what you see. Save battery.

Map and compass

A hiking map with a scale of 1:25,000 is ideal: 1 centimetre on the map is 250 metres in the field. Maps are also available as printouts – print your area beforehand or load offline maps onto your phone.

The needle points north – roughly. It points to the magnetic North Pole. The difference from geographic north is called declination (Missweisung). In Germany it is small: according to Wikipedia it was about one to four degrees east in 2010 and grows by about 0.1 degree every year. At four degrees you drift off by about 70 metres after one kilometre. You can ignore it over short distances. Over long distances, allow for it (look up the value in the map legend or from weather services).

Walking with map and compass

  1. Keep the compass away from metal, magnets, phones and cars. They disturb the needle.
  2. Orient the map to north: Put the compass on the map. Turn map and compass together until the needle points to the north of the map (the map edges point north).
  3. Find your position: Look for landmarks (church, mast, path junction, stream) and compare them with the map.
  4. Take a bearing (direction to the target): Lay the edge of the compass on the line from your position to the target. Turn the housing until the north mark matches map north. Read off the number of degrees.
  5. Walk: Pick a point in that direction (tree, rock) and walk towards it. Then the next one.
  6. Check: Regularly compare the terrain with the map.

You can find a compass tool on this site under Compass. Coordinates and waypoints: Coordinates & GPS.

Emergency compass: A sewing needle or razor blade can be magnetised if you always stroke it in one direction over a magnet. Hang it from a thread or place it on a leaf in a puddle. It then aligns itself roughly north–south.

Finding direction without a compass

All of these methods give only a rough direction. Use more than one and compare.

Shadow stick (best)

  1. Push a straight stick about 1 metre long upright into level ground. It must cast a clear shadow.
  2. Mark the tip of the shadow with a stone. This is west.
  3. Wait 10 to 15 minutes. Mark the new tip of the shadow. This is east.
  4. Draw a line through both marks. This is the west–east line.
  5. Stand with the first mark on your left and the second on your right. You are facing north.

Sun

The sun rises roughly in the east and sets roughly in the west. This is exact only at the equinoxes in spring and autumn. It stands highest in the south. In Germany, the time for this is about 12:00 to 12:40 (winter time) and one hour later in summer time. This is a guide value based on the position within the time zone. You can find sunrise and sunset times in the tool Sun & daylight.

Watch method (rough only)

Hold a watch with hands flat. Point the hour hand at the sun. South lies halfway between the hour hand and the 12 o'clock mark. In summer time use the 1 o'clock mark instead of 12. Wikipedia warns: in Germany the error can be up to 45 degrees depending on the time of day and year. Use this method only if nothing else works.

Stars: the Pole Star

The Pole Star stands almost exactly in the north.

  1. Find the Plough (Big Dipper).
  2. Extend the line through the two rear stars of its bowl about five times.
  3. The Pole Star is there. As a check, it lies at the point in the sky that roughly corresponds to your geographic latitude. In Germany that is roughly 47 to 55 degrees above the horizon.
  4. As a check: the W of Cassiopeia lies on the other side of the Pole Star.

The Plough and Cassiopeia turn around the Pole Star during the night. The Pole Star stays in place.

Moss does not reliably point north

The rule "moss grows on the north side" is not correct. The US manual explicitly rejects it. Moss sometimes grows all the way round the tree. Never use it as the only clue.

Show original – US manual FM 3-05.70, ch. 18

The old saying about using moss on a tree to indicate north is not considered accurate because moss grows completely around some trees.

Finding your way in the city

  • Main roads and trams: They lead to centres. Side streets often lead into residential areas.
  • Rivers and streams: Remember the direction of flow. They are long, straight lines of orientation.
  • High points: Church towers, tall buildings, hills and bridges are visible from far away. Climb a rise if you want to get your bearings.
  • Railway lines and pylons often run straight for a long way.
  • Signs: Street names, house numbers, signposts and stop maps help to work out your position.
  • Sun and shadows also work between buildings. The shadow stick needs an open space for that.
  • Set a meeting point and an escape route early. See Communication & radio.

Estimating distance and counting paces

A double pace (from one foot touching down until the same foot touches down again) measures about 1.5 metres. A single pace in German-speaking countries is usually 71 to 75 centimetres. These are average values, your own measure differs.

How to calibrate yourself:

  1. Measure out a distance of 100 metres (for example on a sports field).
  2. Walk it several times and count the double paces each time (right foot only).
  3. Take the average. This is your measure for 100 metres.

About 65 to 70 double paces are 100 metres. These are estimates: uphill, in mud, in snow or in the dark you will take shorter steps. Allow for an error of about 10 to 20 percent.

Memory aid: Move a stone from one pocket to the other every 100 metres, or tie a knot in a string.

As a guide value (estimate, not a measurement), on flat terrain you cover about 4 kilometres per hour. Groups, luggage, darkness and terrain slow you down.

Noting waypoints

Write down on paper what you see:

  • Time, direction (in degrees), paces or time to the next point
  • Features: "stream, bridge, hunting hide"
  • Sketch: arrows and landmarks

Way back: Add or subtract 180 degrees. If I go out at 40 degrees, I go back at 220 degrees. You can save notes and coordinates in the tool Notes & journal.

Distress signals

If you need help, give conspicuous, regular signals. The US manual names groups of three as a widespread distress signal: three fires, three columns of smoke, three whistles or shots. Nature does not produce groups of three, so they stand out. In alpine terrain, the Alpine distress signal also applies: six signals per minute, one minute pause, then repeat. The reply is three signals per minute. It can be a whistle, shout, flash of light or waving.

  • Whistle: Carries further than shouting. In documented cases, whistles were heard at a distance of around 1.6 kilometres.
  • Light: Flash SOS with a torch or phone light. The tool SOS light helps. Morse code: Morse code.
  • SOS consists of three short, three long and three short signals.
  • Colour: Lay conspicuous clothing or an emergency blanket on the ground.
Behaviour

Give signals until someone answers. Then stay nearby. Save battery and light for the night. Misuse of distress signals is a criminal offence – use them only in a real emergency.
