Power outage guide

What to do in the first minutes, hours and days – and what to pack beforehand.

Does not replace training. In an emergency, call 112 first, or keep trying. This page deliberately gives no medication doses – ask a doctor or pharmacist.

Carbon monoxide: danger to life in enclosed spaces

Never use a barbecue, camping stove, gas stove not approved for indoor use or a generator in the flat, in the cellar or in the garage. They produce an invisible, odourless gas. It can kill. A generator belongs outdoors, according to FEMA (US Federal Emergency Management Agency) at least 20 feet (about 6 m) away from windows, doors and attached garages. Do not heat with the oven or gas hob either. If you have a headache, dizziness or nausea: everyone into the fresh air immediately, call 112 (see First aid).

The first minutes

  1. Check who is affected. Check the fuses in your home. Look out of the window or ask the neighbours: is it only your house without power?
  2. Switch off heating appliances. Cooker, oven, iron, coffee machine, kettle, fan heater. Otherwise they start up unattended when the power comes back. Unplug sensitive devices.
  3. Turn on the radio. A battery or hand-crank radio works without a socket. The car radio helps too. That way you hear announcements from the authorities and the electricity supplier.
  4. Keep the fridge and freezer closed. Open them only if you have to.
  5. Use torches, not candles. Candles need supervision and air to breathe.
  6. Talk to the neighbours. Someone may need help or may be able to help you.
Not an emergency, no emergency call

A power outage alone is not an emergency. Call 112 only in real need. In earlier outages many people called the emergency number for information. That delayed real emergencies. Your grid operator answers questions about how long it will last.

Information and communication

The landline may fail after a few hours. Mobile networks do not have backup power everywhere and quickly become overloaded. So:

  • Make only the calls you really need. That keeps the networks free for police and fire brigade.
  • Send only important messages and save battery where you can.
  • Keep a power bank ready. Charge all batteries as soon as an outage threatens. Solar chargers help too. Use the power & battery tool to work out how long it will last.
  • If 110 and 112 no longer work: in an emergency, go to the nearest police station or hospital if possible.
  • Ask your municipality in advance: where is there information and help? Town halls or emergency meeting points are often contact points.
  • A warning app such as NINA (official German warning app) only helps as long as mobile network and internet work. The radio stays more reliable.

If you need help and nobody hears you: use the SOS light.

Food from the fridge and freezer

According to FEMA, a closed fridge keeps food cold for about 4 hours. A full freezer keeps it for about 48 hours. Measure the temperature if you have a thermometer. Throw away food that has been above about 4 °C for 2 hours or longer, or that smells or looks odd. If in doubt: throw it out. Eat perishable food first.

Medication that must be kept cool: ask your doctor or pharmacist in advance how long it keeps without refrigeration. FEMA advises throwing it away after more than a day without power, unless the package leaflet says otherwise.

Light, warmth and cooking

Light: Use torches or camping lamps. If candles are burning after all, never leave them alone, place them safely and ventilate regularly. Smoke alarms increase safety.

Warmth: Warm clothing, blankets and sleeping bags replace the heating for a while. Stay in one room if possible, keep the doors closed and air the room briefly but thoroughly (opening the windows wide for a short time). Open fireplaces and stoves need wood or coal in stock. Gas heaters only with an oxygen depletion sensor and ignition safety device, and only if they are approved for indoor use. In great cold or heat: ask the authorities about warming or cooling places.

Cooking: Food you can eat cold is simplest. Otherwise:

WhereWhat works
IndoorsTeapot warmer with a tealight, fondue set with fuel paste, gas stove only if approved for indoor use
Outdoors onlyCamping stove with gas cartridge, gas or charcoal barbecue

Have extinguishing equipment ready, such as a fire extinguisher or a fire blanket. Never put out burning fat with water.

Water, money, lift

Water: The tap may stay dry. The BBK (Federal Office for Civil Protection and Disaster Assistance) plans with 2 litres per person per day, of which 0.5 litres for cooking. Calculate your supply: Water needs. Finding water and making it safe: Finding & treating water and Disinfecting water. Without flushing: Hygiene.

Cash: Cash machines and tills stop working. Keep some cash ready.

Lifts and doors: Anything that needs power does not work. Do not use a lift. The BBK also names stopped trains and shops whose tills and doors fail as consequences.

Medical devices and care

Ventilators and home emergency call systems can fail. Discuss with your doctor or care service beforehand what applies in an outage. If you care for relatives, you should be able to look after them yourself for some time if necessary. Also read First aid.

If it lasts longer

Usually the power is back after a few hours. But it can also take days, for example after a storm or snow. Then:

  1. Ration your supplies. Better to plan for more days.
  2. Listen to the radio regularly, but save batteries (e.g. at fixed times).
  3. Exchange information with neighbours. Share cooking places and warmth, but only outdoors or well ventilated.
  4. Keep up hygiene, otherwise illness threatens: Hygiene & sanitation.
  5. When the power returns, switch devices back on one after another. Check the food from the fridge and freezer.

Preparation: the checklist

The BBK recommends being able to look after yourself for 10 days. Even 3 days of supplies help a lot. Build up step by step.
